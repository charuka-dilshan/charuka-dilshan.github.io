Introduction to Multimedia: Fundamentals & History (CO1224 Lecture 01)

An exhaustive study guide covering multimedia definitions, components, research topics, analog vs. digital media history, hypermedia, and milestones in the new millennium.

Academic Note: This master study guide covers Chapter 1 (“Introduction to Multimedia”) of Fundamentals of Multimedia (3rd Edition) by Ze-Nian Li, Mark S. Drew, and Jiangchuan Liu.

Module 1: What is Multimedia?

Depending on who you ask, the term “multimedia” has different viewpoints:

  • Consumer Entertainment Vendor: Interactive TV with hundreds of digital channels, cable-like services over high-speed internet, or a smartphone.
  • Computer Science (CS) Student / Researcher: Applications that use multiple modalities, including text, images, drawings (graphics), animation, video, sound (including speech), and interactivity.

1.1 Formal Definition

  • General Definition: Multimedia is the field concerned with the computer-controlled integration of text, graphics, drawings, still and moving images (video), animation, audio, and any other media where every type of information can be represented, stored, transmitted, and processed digitally.
  • Multimedia Application: An application that uses a collection of multiple media sources (e.g., text, graphics, images, sound/audio, animation, and/or video).

1.2 Components of Multimedia (Modalities)

Multimedia involves multiple modalities put to use in various advanced systems:

  1. Video-conferencing & Tele-medicine.
  2. Cloud-based web video editors for editing and remixing professional videos.
  3. Real-time augmented reality (AR) and massively multiplayer online video games.
  4. Shapeshifting TV (viewers vote on plot paths) and inverse-Hollywood applications.
  5. Large-scale video/image database semantic searching.
  6. Hybrid compositing of artificial and natural video.

Module 2: Multimedia Research Topics & Interdisciplinary Nature

To computer scientists, multimedia research spans three major pillars:

  1. Multimedia Processing and Coding: Content analysis, content-based retrieval, security, audio/image/video processing, and compression.
  2. Multimedia System Support and Networking: Network protocols, the Internet, operating systems, servers/clients, Quality of Service (QoS), and databases.
  3. Multimedia Tools, End-Systems, and Applications: Hypermedia systems, user interfaces, authoring systems, multimodal interaction, “ubiquity” (web-everywhere devices), and virtual environments.

2.1 Interdisciplinary Connections

  • Electrical Engineering: Signal processing for audio/video signals.
  • Physics: Color science in images and video.
  • Medicine & Psychology: Human perception of multimedia data.

Module 3: Multimedia: Past and Present

3.1 Early History (Analog Era)

All early mass communication media were in analog format, where the time-varying signal feature is a continuous representation (analogous) to the input signal:

  1. Newspaper (Text, graphics, images): Perhaps the first mass communication medium.
  2. Motion Pictures (1830s): Conceived to observe motion too rapid for perception by the human eye.
  3. Wireless Radio Transmission (1895): Guglielmo Marconi in Pontecchio, Italy.
  4. Television (20th Century): Established video as a widely available mass medium.
  • Note on Audio Storage: Evolved from Edison’s 1877 phonograph cylinder to flat vinyl records, reel-to-reel magnetic tape, cassette tapes, and compact discs (CDs).

3.2 Hypermedia, WWW, and Internet Timeline

  • 1945: Vannevar Bush wrote a landmark article describing Memex (early hypermedia).
  • 1965: Ted Nelson coined the term hypertext (non-linear text containing links).
  • 1968: Douglas Engelbart demonstrated the On-Line System (NLS).
  • 1989: Tim Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web (WWW).
  • 1991: MPEG-1 approved; introduction of PDAs.
  • 1992: JPEG image compression standard accepted.
  • 1993: NCSA Mosaic released (first full-fledged web browser).
  • 1995: Java language created for platform-independent development.
  • 1996: DVD video introduced.

3.3 Linear vs. Non-Linear Presentation

  • Linear Presentation: Progresses without navigational control for the viewer (e.g., cinema).
  • Non-Linear / Interactive: Uses interactivity to control progress (e.g., video games, hypermedia).

3.4 Markup Languages

  • HTML (HyperText Markup Language): Published via ASCII for portability across hardware architectures (Current standard: HTML5).
  • XML (Extensible Markup Language): Separates data structure and view, allowing users/applications to define custom tags.

Module 4: Multimedia in the New Millennium (2000–Present)

  • 2004: Web 2.0 promotes user collaboration (Facebook, Flickr founded).
  • 2005: YouTube created; Google Maps launched.
  • 2006: Twitter created; Amazon cloud computing launched; Nintendo Wii released (3D movement detection).
  • 2007: Apple iPhone (iOS) and Google Android launched.
  • 2010: Netflix migrates to cloud; Microsoft Kinect introduced (3D motion & voice capture).
  • 2013: 4K TVs available; YouTube supports 360-degree videos; AlphaGo beats human professional.
  • 2016: Microsoft HoloLens (mixed reality); Pokémon Go popularizes AR.
  • 2018: 16K Ultra HD video; 5G deployment; Wi-Fi 6 released.
  • 2020: COVID-19 pandemic drives booming remote work/study tools (Zoom, Microsoft Teams, Google Classroom).

Module 5: Multimedia Software Tools

Core software tools used in multimedia development include:

  1. Music sequencing and notation software.
  2. Digital audio editors.
  3. Graphics and image editing suites.
  4. Video editing software.
  5. Animation tools.
  6. Multimedia authoring and broadcasting systems.

Quick Study Summary Checklist

  • Define multimedia as the computer-controlled digital integration of text, graphics, images, video, animation, and audio.
  • Understand the 3 main CS research pillars: Processing/Coding, Systems/Networking, and Tools/Applications.
  • Distinguish between analog media (continuous representation) and digital media (binary format).
  • Explain the difference between linear presentations (cinema) and non-linear/interactive presentations (games, hype rmedia).
  • Know the historical milestones: Ted Nelson (Hypertext, 1965), Tim Berners-Lee (WWW, 1989), and NCSA Mosaic (1993).
